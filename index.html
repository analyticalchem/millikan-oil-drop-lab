About the Model

Spray a mist of oil into the chamber, pick a drop, and time it falling under gravity and rising in the electric field between the plates. The fall gives the drop's radius and mass, each timed rise gives its charge, and the charges come in steps: the charge of one electron.

Using the page

The Apparatus page is the experiment. The toolbar above the view switches the plate voltage and the ionization source, sprays drops, pauses, looks into the eyepiece and tracks drops; Settings holds the plate voltage slider with its typing box, and the speed of the simulation. See the results opens this page, which follows the experiment as it runs; Back to the apparatus, or the browser's Back button, returns to it.

In the cutaway you can work the apparatus directly: click the atomizer bulb to spray, the knife switch on the supply to turn the field on or off, Raise and Lower beside the voltmeter to change the voltage (hold to keep going), the lever on the α source to expose or shield it, and the eyepiece to look into it. Drag to turn the model, or focus it and use the arrow keys; hold Ctrl and scroll (or pinch) to zoom, or press + and −; Whole Apparatus and Chamber jump between views; Cutaway puts the missing quarter of the chamber back. The magnified view at the top right shows the gap between the plates. In either view, click a drop to track it, or use the Previous Drop and Next Drop buttons; the tracked drop is ringed in both views. The scope shows an upright image: drops that fall move down.

The experiment

Robert Millikan (1909 to 1913) sprayed oil into the gap between two horizontal metal plates, watched single drops through a scope, and timed them. With the plates shorted a drop falls at a steady speed set by its weight and by the air's drag; with a voltage across the plates a charged drop is pushed up (or down) as well, and moves at a different steady speed. From the two speeds the drop's radius, mass and charge follow. Charging the drops again with ionizing radiation, Millikan found the charge always changed by a whole number of one unit.

Working the apparatus

  1. Spray Oil Drops, or click the atomizer bulb in the cutaway. The bulb blows a mist from the nozzle into the space above the top plate, and the puff of air carries the drops through the hole in the plate into the gap; the rest land on the plate. Look into the eyepiece and pick one that falls slowly (a small drop) and is sharp at the current focus.
  2. Track it by clicking it, or with the Previous Drop and Next Drop buttons. Keep the source off while timing.
  3. Time its fall with the plates shorted, across a known number of long reticle lines (0.5 mm apart). The Drop Properties table then shows its radius and mass.
  4. Switch the plate voltage on. The top plate is positive and the bottom plate negative, so a negatively charged drop (most of them) rises; time its rise. A positively charged drop falls faster instead, and its charge follows just the same. Time a fall and a rise more than once if you can.
  5. Change its charge. Expose the ionization source for a few seconds, then time the rise again. A violet ring spreads from a drop at the moment it captures an ion; with the field on you then see it rise or fall at a new speed, and with the field off nothing else changes until the field goes on, because a drop's fall does not depend on its charge. Repeat for several charges on the same drop, and for several drops.
  6. Search for Unit of Charge. All the charges should be whole-number multiples of one value.

Reset Experiment clears the drops, the clock and every measurement and puts the controls back to their starting values, leaving the view as it is; it asks first when there are measurements to lose.

The calculation

A drop of radius r and effective density ρ (oil less air) falling at speed vf has its weight balanced by Stokes drag, corrected for the fact that such small drops slip between the air molecules:

(4⁄3) π r³ ρ g = 6 π η r vf ÷ (1 + b ⁄ (p r))

which gives the radius from the fall speed, and the mass as m = (4⁄3) π r³ ρ. With the field E = V ⁄ d on and the drop moving at velocity v (up positive), the electric force makes up the difference, so that the charge is

|q| = m g (1 + v ⁄ vf) d ⁄ V

For a drop that rises at speed vr this is the familiar form (vf + vr) ⁄ vf; a drop that still falls with the field on, only more slowly, gives a charge the same way with v negative. Because the field always points down, a drop that falls faster with the field on than off is positively charged, and the table says so.

Constants used: g = 9.80 m/s², ρ = 886 − 1.2 kg/m³, d = 14.92 mm (the gap in Millikan's 1913 condenser), b = 8.20 × 10⁻³ Pa·m, p = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa, and the viscosity of air at the chamber's 22.0 °C, η = 1.82 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s. The Charge Calculation tab works through these steps with numbers you type in from the Measurements table, so the same arithmetic the page does for every row can be followed by hand.

Finding the unit

The page works out |q| for every timed run with the field on, using the mean fall speed of the same drop. Search for Unit of Charge scans trial values from 4 × 10⁻¹⁹ C downward and takes the largest one whose multiples fit the charges about as well as any unit does (a local minimum of the root-mean-square deviation, within 2.5 times the best), then refines it by least squares and slides the trial unit to it. With only one or two charges almost anything fits: the search needs several drops, or one drop at several charges, and small charges (a few units) are far more useful than large ones, whose errors swamp the steps. With the instructor check on, the search uses the true charges of the measured drops instead of the measured values, which shows what the method gives with perfect timings. The accepted value is e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

What the simulation does

  • Each drop has a radius between about 0.35 and 1.3 µm, a charge of a few elementary units (three quarters of them negative), and moves at its terminal velocity, recomputed every frame. A fresh drop rides the puff of air from the bulb: swept toward the 0.8 mm hole in the top plate, through it, and a few millimetres down into the gap before the stream dies away.
  • Drops jitter with Brownian motion of the right size for their radius and the air temperature, which is why repeated timings of the same drop differ slightly, and why very small drops are hard to time.
  • With the source on, each drop captures an air ion about every two seconds, changing its charge by one unit (sometimes two) of either sign. With the source off, a drop's charge never changes.
  • A slow convection current drifts the drops sideways; drops that reach a plate or drift out of the chamber are lost.
  • The scope has a shallow depth of field: only drops near the focal plane are sharp. It shows an upright image; some real scopes invert it.
  • The cutaway is a 3D model of Millikan's apparatus, after the original at Caltech, with a quarter of the chamber cut away. The chamber, scope, lamp and atomizer are at about their true size, and the plates are 14.92 mm apart, as in Millikan's 1913 condenser. At that scale a drop is far too small to see, so the magnified view of the gap draws the drops hundreds of times larger than life. The eyepiece shows the drops at their true relative positions, each as the point of light a real drop scatters.
  • The clock, the stopwatch and the automatic timer all run on simulated time, so the Speed setting changes nothing but your waiting.
  • The automatic timer works while you look through the eyepiece. It notes the moment the tracked drop crosses each long reticle line and records a measurement each time the drop has covered the Distance timed set beside the stopwatch, in one direction under one field setting; a shorter stretch, cut off by a change of field, a change of direction or the drop leaving the view, is not recorded, and timing pauses while the source is on.

Keyboard

Tab moves between controls; arrow keys move the sliders and, on the row of tabs, between the tabs. Apparatus and Results at the top switch between the two pages. The Plate voltage box in Settings takes an exact value (Enter applies it, Escape abandons the edit, and a second Escape closes Settings). With the 3D view focused, the arrow keys turn the model and + and − zoom it. Previous Drop and Next Drop track drops without the mouse, and every reading is in the caption under the view and in the tables. Escape closes Settings, then the chart's tooltip, then presentation mode. Alt + Shift + P (Option + Shift + P on a Mac) switches presentation mode on and off.

About this page

Millikan Oil Drop Lab is an interactive teaching page: a simulated oil drop apparatus with a 3D cutaway and an eyepiece view, timing, and the analysis that gives each drop's radius, mass and charge and from them the charge of the electron. Plates 14.92 mm apart, oil density 886 kg/m³, air at 22.0 °C.
